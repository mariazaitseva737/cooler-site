## Why it happens

As estrogen drops, the part of your brain that controls body temperature becomes more sensitive. It mistakes a tiny rise in temperature for overheating and switches on cooling: blood vessels widen, your face and chest flush, you sweat. Once the wave passes, you may feel chilly.

At night the same thing wakes you up. You wake drenched, throw off the covers, then get cold. That is why hot flashes so often wreck sleep.

## How long it lasts

In the large US study SWAN, hot flashes lasted 7.4 years on average. Women whose hot flashes started before their periods stopped usually had them longer. Women whose hot flashes started only after their last period had them for about three and a half years on average. For some women they go on for more than ten years.

So “just wait, it will pass” is poor advice. If hot flashes are getting in the way of your life, they are worth treating.

## What the research supports

- Hormone therapy reduces hot flashes the most. For most healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, the benefits usually outweigh the risks, but the decision is always individual.
- If hormones are not right for you or you do not want them, there are prescription non-hormonal options. Ask your doctor about them.
- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) helps hot flashes bother you less. In studies, clinical hypnosis also reduced how often they happened.
- If you are carrying extra weight, losing some of it may also reduce hot flashes.

## What helps in the moment

A cool room, layers, water nearby and a slow exhale do not reduce the number of hot flashes. The Menopause Society’s 2023 guidance says so too. They do make the moment easier to get through without panicking. That is what the “Hot flash started” button in the app is for.

Coffee, alcohol, spicy food and heat trigger hot flashes for some women and not for others. If you want to check your own triggers, log them for a couple of weeks. You will see whether there is a link for you.
