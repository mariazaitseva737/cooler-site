## Where the fear comes from

In 2002, the first results of the large WHI study frightened doctors and women alike, and many stopped taking hormones. Later the data were looked at more closely. Most participants were over 60, and many started therapy long after menopause. For younger women closer to menopause, the picture turned out to be different.

## Who it may suit

It usually comes up when hot flashes and night sweats are hard to live with, or when the risk of bone loss is high. The balance of benefit and risk is best for women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause. Starting later is possible, but the risks get weighed more carefully.

## When extra caution is needed

If you have had breast cancer, blood clots, a stroke or a heart attack, have liver disease or unexplained bleeding, tell your doctor right at the start. In these cases the decision is made especially carefully, and other options are often chosen.

## Local therapy is different

For vaginal dryness and frequent urinary infections, doctors use local treatments with very low doses of hormones. Very little reaches the bloodstream, and the rules for them are different from regular HRT. More on this in the [article on intimate health](/en/blog/vaginal-dryness-after-menopause/).

## What to ask your doctor

- Is HRT right for me, given my health history and my family’s?
- Which form suits me best: pills, a patch or a gel?
- Do I need a progestogen? You usually do if you still have your uterus.
- How long should I take it, and how will we decide when to stop?
- Which tests do I need before starting and during treatment?
